Operations Research project

What to charge, and who to call.

Two optimisation engines on IBM's Telco churn data (7,043 customers). A nonlinear program sets the prices of ten services without letting churn rise; an integer program picks which at-risk customers to call with which of six retention offers. A sweep then shows how retention spending frees room to raise prices.

What's new · v1.2

Easier to read, honest about slow servers, runs anywhere

An Index for every term

A new page explains every term, formula and method in plain language, with the agent time and cost of each offer read straight from the engine. A "?" beside any label jumps to its entry. The retention budget is now typed as an exact amount.

Honest about slow solves

If the solver stops at its time limit, the page says so instead of presenting a cut-short answer as the best. Long runs show what they are solving and for how long, Engine 2 warns when a background run shares the server, and a run lost to a restart says so.

Repeat runs are instant

Asking for a sweep or robustness check with the same settings as a finished one returns it at once, and an identical run that is already going is joined instead of started twice, which matters on a small shared server.

Runs anywhere

One Docker image holds the whole app, it is live on a free cloud server, and every change is tested automatically on Windows and on Linux. Light and dark themes, an animated logo and a slow "signal network" background round it off.

How it works

Two engines and the link between them

Engine 1 · nonlinear program

Price optimizer

Sets one price for each of ten services to maximise customer lifetime profit, where, in the model, each customer's monthly churn risk rises with their bill.

  • Each price within ±25%; no bill moves more than 15%
  • Fibre stays $10 above DSL
  • Average churn may not rise above today's
  • SciPy SLSQP from 8 starting points; all agree
Engine 2 · binary integer program

Retention optimizer

For each of 1,651 customers with a monthly churn risk of 3% or more, at most one of six offers: contracts, free services, an autopay switch.

  • 9,124 eligible customer-offer pairs
  • Budget, agent hours and a cap per offer
  • SciPy milp (HiGHS); within 0.01% of the LP bound
  • Beats the best of three greedy rankings by 5.16%
Coupling

Retention buys pricing room

Calling at-risk customers lowers their churn, which leaves room under Engine 1's churn ceiling. The sweep runs Engine 2 at each budget, then Engine 1 on the lower churn.

  • Budgets from $0 to $90,000
  • Net gain after retention spend
  • Runs as a background job with progress

Churn risk comes from a piecewise-exponential hazard model (Poisson GLM on 227,990 customer-months). Prices were recovered from the bills by least squares (R² 0.9988).

What the engines find

Prices

Add-ons rise to their +25% bound, the phone line 12.5%, and fibre comes down 17.9%: that cut is what the churn ceiling requires. Without the ceiling every price simply rises 15%. Revenue +3.5%, modelled lifetime profit +7.6%.

The pattern is identical for price sensitivity anywhere from 0.4 to 1.5; only the size of the fibre cut moves (−17.6% to −18.3%). Each extra 1% of churn the business would accept is worth $164,816.

Who to call

$30,000, 500 agent hours, at most 350 customers per offer: 901 customers called (350 autopay, 349 one-year, 198 two-year, 4 online security) for an expected $182,949.

Re-solved with contract effects cut to half or a quarter, or acceptance at 15% or 50%: about 80-95% of the same customers are still called. Which offer they get is less stable, so the call list is the firm output.

Operations Research methods

The models behind the answers

MethodWhere it is used
Nonlinear programming (managerial economics)Engine 1: pricing where churn rises with the bill through a price elasticity η; solved with SLSQP from eight starting points
Integer programmingEngine 2: 9,124 yes/no call decisions, branch and bound (HiGHS) to a 0.1% optimality gap
Linear programmingEngine 2's linear relaxation gives the upper bound the call list is measured against; lifetime value is a discounting model
Assignment model with side constraintsCustomers assigned to offers: at most one each, a capacity per offer, plus the budget and agent hours
Knapsack and capital budgetingThe budget limit is a knapsack constraint; choosing calls is capital budgeting over thousands of small projects
Sensitivity analysisShadow price of the churn ceiling ($164,816 per 1%); binding constraints; re-solves across price sensitivity, contract effects, acceptance and budget

The budget sweep reads like a falling shadow price: each extra retention dollar is worth about $69 of net gain up to $15,000, $54 up to $30,000, then $4 and under $1, as money stops being the scarce resource.

Limits

What this work does not claim

  • No measured price sensitivity. No price ever changed in the data, so how customers react to price is a scenario input, tested from 0.4 to 1.5, not an estimate.
  • No measured offer effects. Customers on two-year contracts churn far less, but they chose those contracts. The model's ratios are associations, so the app re-solves with them cut to half and a quarter.
  • Expected values, not forecasts. $182,949 assumes 30% of customers accept (a literature prior) and that an offer's effect lasts its term.
  • The churn ceiling drives the price answer, not the shape of the profit curve.
  • Greedy is not as good. Ranking customers by score is 4-6% worse than solving the integer program.

Six figures in an earlier version of this project's deck and report were contradicted by the data (for example "75% of month-to-month customers left": the data says 42.7%). The automated checks keep those as visible failures.

  • Browser test (Playwright, 25 steps) in Chromium and WebKit
